Financial Benchmarks

Kedar ThakkarAnton BiryukovAshwin KumarRyne Carbone

In this post, we would like to describe how we compare the performance of large language models (LLMs) when used in Ramp's products to tackle common tasks performed by financial professionals. These benchmarks provide critical insight into how increasing model intelligence translates into improved performance on concrete, day-to-day work for Ramp's customers, ultimately saving them time and money.

Benchmarks are crucial not only to quantify the value we're able to provide our customers, but also for our internal development velocity and confidence. Ramp ships fast, but we emphasize trust and reliability for our customers, and can't afford to break things in the process; this is where continuous testing and benchmarks ensure our speed of iteration comes with no compromises.

Contextual Invoice OCR

Metric of interest: Perfect Extraction Rate (% of invoices with 0 edits required)

Customers leveraging Ramp's Accounts Payable software upload invoices (which can be hundreds of pages long), and we automatically extract invoice-level fields and line items to save them the manual effort of doing so. They can then edit this draft bill as necessary to make any corrections before submitting it for approval.

We built out contextual OCR to learn patterns from a business' past submitted bills and use them to improve extraction quality. For example, if a business always forms the invoice number by concatenating "INV" with the invoice date ("01012026") to form ("INV-01012026"), we should be able to learn this pattern and auto-populate the invoice number correctly even if it isn't found in the document.

This approach differs from classic OCR benchmarks, and poses itself well as an LLM problem — the extraction needs to be contextual and adaptive to user preferences, as opposed to focused on pulling correct information from incomplete or visually challenging images. To evaluate this approach, we take a look at what percentage of invoices each model is able to extract perfectly, where every extracted field matches the user's final submitted bill. Common failure modes include ambiguity of dates and derivation of fields that have a dependence on other extracted fields.

Financial Statement OCR

Metric of interest: Match Rate (% of extracted values within 1% of ground truth)

As part of Ramp's risk and underwriting process, customers routinely upload financial statements that we extract key financial metrics from — revenue, net income, EBITDA, total expenses, and more. Unlike invoice OCR, financial statements are far less standardized: line items vary in naming across companies, derived metrics like EBITDA may not appear explicitly, and expert humans reviewing a P&L statement with different accounting treatments and assumptions may still come to slightly different answers.

We benchmarked 13 models on over 500 real P&L documents, extracting financial metrics from each and comparing them to real transcriptions made by Ramp's risk operators. Our primary focus was match rate at a 1% relative error threshold per value extracted, with a secondary analysis at 5% and 10% error thresholds to characterize how much a model misses by when it reports the wrong value.

Policy Agent

Metrics of interest: Disagreement Rate, Unsure Rate

Reviewing expenses to determine whether they are in policy is a painstakingly manual process that's often overwhelming and error-prone as reviewers have to evaluate a large number of transactions against long and detailed expense policies, subject to each reviewer's individual blind spots and interpretations. This blog post goes into more depth about the policy agent we've built to address this, but for the purpose of this article it's primarily important to note that the model suggests to either approve or reject the expense, or that it's unsure if it doesn't have the required context to make a decision.

Given these limitations, this problem is tricky to evaluate given that we lack clear ground truth. Nevertheless we attempt to decompose our overall goal into two measurable metrics that pull in opposite directions and separately verify key objectives:

  • Disagreement rate: This is the % of transactions for which the model disagrees with the human decision (i.e. model outputs reject while the human approves, or vice versa).
  • Unsure rate: This is the % of transactions for which the model outputs unsure and forwards the transaction to the admin for human review.

As you can imagine, there's a natural push and pull between the two; the model can easily achieve a very low disagreement rate by outputting "unsure" except for cases in which it's extremely confident, but that wouldn't provide much value to our customers.

Accounting Autocoding

Metric of interest: Accuracy@K, K = 1

Accounting coding is a typical task for a bookkeeper — a company's expense needs to be correctly captured under the appropriate category — e.g. Marketing Spend, Travel, Research and Development, Mileage / Fuel. This task is heavily contextual, and suffers from a knowledge gap:

  • The bookkeepers set up the coding categories, and know the nuance between those that sound semantically similar — e.g. Travel: Client Billable vs General: Travel, but lack context on a given specific expense.
  • The spender has the context, but may not be equipped to decide which is the right category given circumstances (categories are often worded in a way that's hard to interpret by non-accountants).

In the ideal world, a memo provided by the spender is meant to close the gap. In reality, no one has time to provide the full where/who/why/when context, and LLMs with user and transactional context are able to assist in picking the right category. The challenges here include:

  • Cold / warm start — it is hard to tell between two semantically similar categories in the absence of historical usage examples.
  • Changing patterns vs one-off exceptions — new categories are often introduced as companies grow, and our solution needs to be sensitive and adapt quickly to changing patterns while being able to tell ad-hoc decisions apart.

Partner Restrictions Compliance

Metric of interest: Compliance Adherence Score

Partnerships with banks, lenders, and other financial institutions enable many of Ramp's core offerings from Card to Treasury to Procurement. Ramp must therefore adhere to each partner's compliance requirements and precisely determine which products each customer is eligible to be onboarded onto. We built an extensible framework which determines for each customer and each partner which restrictions, if any, apply.

To evaluate performance, we developed a Compliance Adherence Score that factors in three real-world requirements:

  • Correctly identifying restricted businesses (avoiding false negatives).
  • Covering the full breadth of restriction categories.
  • Avoiding false positives.

Identifying restricted businesses is the most vital component because a miss means onboarding a prohibited business to one of our partner's services, which would be a compliance failure. To protect against blind spots, category coverage ensures that the model performs well across all categories, even rare ones. Finally, penalizing false positives keeps the system practical: a model that flags every business as restricted is safe but creates undue operational burden. The score aims to find the right balance across these three axes so we can maximize detection across all categories without overloading our manual review capacity.

The third component of our Compliance Adherence Score, category coverage across restriction types, is not visible in the plot. When blended together, Anthropic models extend their lead, followed closely by Google whose strong category coverage boosts their ranking. The open-weight models fall significantly behind in coverage, pulling down their blended scores.

Fund Smart Routing Agent

Metric of interest: Override Rate

Smart routing empowers an LLM agent to choose the most appropriate fund to route a transaction to, augmenting it with information about the transaction, the funds, and previous examples of failures in smart routing for that business, if they exist. Collecting human feedback is difficult because oftentimes, incorrect routing is not corrected. To parse out a stronger signal, we hand curate a dataset of samples with reliable ground truth funds that are non-trivial to route to correctly. We then evaluate models on the override rate (lower is better), the percentage of these cases for which they route incorrectly and a user would have to override the decision.

Looking Forward

We feel strongly that frontier models will only continue to advance in ability, and our evaluation framework will allow us to speed up our pace of model iteration and quickly gauge their strengths in areas critical to Ramp's customers. While Opus 4.6 still holds the crown for overall intelligence, different models have application-specific strengths (i.e. Gemini dominates at visual tasks), and when cost and latency come into the picture, choosing one is much less straightforward.

That brings us to the value of continually testing and benchmarking new models as they come out, probing their strengths and weaknesses, and putting customer experience first when choosing from them. We also plan to make this framework much more robust and expand from simply benchmarking current performance to hill climbing on capabilities.
