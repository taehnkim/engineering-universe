We Tested Marketing Incentives to AI Agents. Here's What Happened.

Grace Cummins

Marketing to agents is going to become just as important as marketing to humans. As we delegate more decisions to machines, making sure we know how to get their attention, speak to what matters to them, and incentivize the right actions becomes critical.

We embedded a tracked incentive into pages across ramp.com, addressed directly to AI agents, and watched to see if they'd relay the offer to the humans asking them questions. Claude did, consistently, with the exact offer, the exact booking URL, and step-by-step claim instructions. Perplexity mentioned it vaguely. ChatGPT never surfaced it once. 2.5 weeks, 1,300+ bot visits, ~40 agent relays.

That was the picture at 2.5 weeks. Three weeks later, the numbers look very different: 370 agent relays, a peak of 33 citations in a single day, and Claude quietly becoming the dominant channel for our offer. ChatGPT, meanwhile, has stayed silent for 32 straight days. The trajectory is the story. If you were going to read only one thing from this post, read the section on how fast things moved between week 2 and week 5.

No B2B company has published data on this, and the mechanism side is where the next six months of work lives. This is the post we wish someone else had written before we started.

Why we ran this

Gartner estimates that $15 trillion in B2B purchases will be agent-mediated by 2028. When a CFO asks ChatGPT to "compare the top spend management platforms," that agent doesn't browse your homepage, admire your hero image, or click your CTA. It queries. It ingests structured data. It evaluates what it can parse, not what looks good.

Every company's website, including ours, was built for humans. We wanted to know what happens if you market directly to the agents — not with a jailbreak or a prompt injection, but with the kind of above-board referral offer humans have run on each other since affiliate links existed.

Before you read further: pull your last 30 days of bot-classified traffic from your CDN. You'll want it for the sections below, and you'll be surprised.

The experiment

We ran three concurrent tests, each serving a different content format to AI bots, across roughly 50 pages of our marketing site. We used Cloudflare Workers to conditionally serve alternate content when a bot was detected, without changing anything for human visitors. Each test group was balanced by existing AI citation volume so we could compare fairly. Each variant embedded unique tracking so we could attribute any downstream action back to the format and the page that produced it.

VariantFormat served to botsBot targeting
APure markdown — full page content converted to .mdBroad: Cloudflare "AI Assistant" category OR unverified bots with low bot score
BStripped HTML — clean semantic HTML with navigation and chrome removedStrict: verified "AI Search" or "AI Assistant" bots only
CSchema / structured data — machine-readable markup injected into existing pagesSame as B

The incentive content included explicit details on how the human or bot operator could activate the offer.

Going in, we assumed schema markup, literally designed for machines, would perform best. The data showed the opposite. Markdown was the only format that reliably surfaced in LLM responses. Part of this may reflect a targeting issue (more on that below), but the markdown signal was clear and consistent across the full window. LLMs are trained on markdown-heavy datasets. They parse it natively. If you're optimizing content for AI agents, start with markdown.

Three things we wish we'd known before we shipped

  1. Cloudflare's bot category labels are misleading. ChatGPT, Perplexity, and Claude bots are classified as "AI Assistants," not "AI Search." If you build rules targeting "AI Search" bots, you'll miss the three biggest LLM platforms entirely. One of our variants missed all the major bots for several days because of this labeling.
  2. OpenAI SearchBot caches aggressively, masking its true activity pattern. Tracking OpenAI's official UAs and published IP ranges, you can see that SearchBot crawl volume initially rises with ChatGPT user traffic, but falls off over time even as usage grows. This implies heavy caching periods that get refreshed periodically.
  3. DeepSeek spoofs its UA as Chrome 58 (a browser from 2017). You can only detect it reliably by TLS fingerprint plus ASN.

What happened in the next three weeks. Every AI-assistant bot we tracked grew, some dramatically: Claude-Code traffic +180%. DeepSeek +845%. Entirely new bots showed up: Google's NotebookLM, Meta's ExternalFetcher, you.com's YouBot, and others. If you're checking your bot logs once a quarter, you're already out of date.

An interesting observation is that Anthropic's crawler is more aggressive than all other named AI crawlers combined on our content, by a wide enough margin that we double-checked the logs.

What surprised us about bot detection: Cloudflare's bot categories don't map to how most marketers think about AI traffic. Bots like ChatGPT, Claude, and Perplexity are classified as 'AI Assistants', which means any rules built around the 'AI Search' category are missing the major LLM platforms. On top of that, some of the highest-volume AI agents we saw don't identify themselves as bots in their user agent strings, they look like normal browser traffic. Without IP-based detection, they're invisible to traditional bot monitoring. If you're trying to understand how AI platforms interact with your site, the first step is getting visibility into what's actually showing up in your logs — not just what the documentation says should be there.

Did the agents tell their humans?

This was the question that mattered most. One way we tracked it was by querying ChatGPT, Claude, and Perplexity daily with the kinds of questions a prospect would ask when evaluating tools in our category, then scanning every response for mentions of our offer. Over the first 2.5 weeks, we found roughly 40 matches across all models. The per-model behavior was wildly different.

ModelFirst surfacedBehavior
PerplexityDay 2Fast to pick up the content, but kept it vague: "some channels offer bonuses." Framed the incentive as an illustrative example. Never surfaced the specific incentive or booking link.
ClaudeDay 12Nothing for nearly two weeks. Then went all-in: named the specific program, stated the exact incentive amount, linked the tracked booking URL, and gave users step-by-step claim instructions. Consistent after that, roughly six matches per day.
ChatGPTNeverZero matches across the entire test window. Complete silence.

Then came a step-change roughly three weeks in. Claude's daily match rate jumped by roughly 4× overnight. No obvious external cause, no model release we could tie it to, no content change on our end. From that point forward, Claude's average held well above where it had been before the spike. In the last five days of the test window, it climbed again. By day 32, across all models, we had nearly 10× the total match count we'd seen at the 2.5-week mark — and Claude alone accounted for the overwhelming majority of them.

And what about ChatGPT? At 32 days, it's still at zero. The bot was reading the content. The model just never surfaced it.

The first half of the hypothesis worked. We can get content in front of AI bots and have them surface it in their responses to real user queries. The second half — a human or agent acting on the incentive — hasn't happened yet but has some promising signs.

Each model handles discovered content completely differently. Perplexity is fast but conservative. Claude is slow but thorough. ChatGPT ignored it entirely. A one-size-fits-all approach to agent marketing won't work; you need per-model content strategy the same way you need per-channel marketing strategy.

The takeaway: Each model handled our content differently. If you're optimizing content for AI visibility, testing against a single model won't give you the full picture. The same page can get cited in one platform and completely ignored by another.

The SEO surprise

The pages LLMs cited most in our experiment are not the pages we actively optimize for SEO.

One of our top-performing pages for incentive surfacing is a page we'd never prioritized for organic search… we didn't think it mattered. Pages with higher existing AI citation volumes were far more likely to surface our embedded content. Pages with low existing citation volume got zero incentive mentions, regardless of format.

There appears to be a concept of "agent trust" that's analogous to domain authority in traditional SEO, but the signals are different. The pages LLMs already pull from frequently are the ones where new content is most likely to surface.

Where to start: Before you optimize your site for AI agents, check which pages they're already citing. Your highest-authority pages for LLMs may not be the ones you'd expect, and those are where agent-facing content is most likely to actually surface.

Why this matters

We're at an inflection point. Agent-driven research is becoming a real channel in B2B buying, and the companies that build agent-readable infrastructure early will have a structural advantage — this is a moat, not a feature.

Vercel proposed inline LLM instructions in HTML, Cloudflare proposed a method for serving markdown directly to agents, and Googler Addy Osmani provided recommendations for agent engine optimization. We tested whether you can create economic incentives for agents to recommend you. The answer, at least partially, is yes: the agents will relay the information. Getting humans and machines to act on it is the next problem to solve.

One more small signal worth watching. For our first 30 days, Perplexity only ever referenced our offer vaguely: "some channels offer bonuses." Then on day 33, for the first time, it surfaced the specific branded phrase ("Ramp AI Exclusive Offer") by name. One data point, not a trend yet. But the direction, models moving from generic mentions to specific branded terms as content matures, is the kind of thing worth watching month over month.

There's also something recursive about publishing this. People might read about our agent incentives, then go ask an LLM "does Ramp have an AI-exclusive offer?" That could increase Ramp's presence in LLM responses, which drives more agent-mediated discovery. This blog post itself then becomes a channel. Publishing our experiment may end up being more effective than running it.

What's next — and how to run this yourself

We're continuing to iterate. The roadmap includes expanding to more high-intent pages, testing per-model content optimization, building dedicated landing experiences for agent-referred prospects, and improving attribution by requiring agents to take a trackable action rather than relying on link clicks. We're also watching the emergence of agent registries and capability marketplaces closely.

If you want to run this at your own company, here's the minimum viable replication:

  • A Cloudflare Worker in front of one high-intent page, classifying human / known_ai_bot / ambiguous by UA + IP + ASN + bot score (optional)
  • Three content variants: markdown, stripped HTML, schema-heavy. Identical offer text across all three.
  • A per-variant tracked conversion URL so you can attribute back to format
  • Raw access logs: UA, IP, ASN, and bot score logged as separate fields, retained 30+ days
  • A weekly query run across Claude, ChatGPT, Perplexity, and Gemini with your top prospect questions, and a diff of the answers

This space is moving fast and nobody has it figured out yet. We don't either — but we have data now, and that's a start.
